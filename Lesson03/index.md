## Lesson 3 Designing Statistical Studies
Now that we know how to gather a random sample, we need to decide how we're actually going to collect data from that sample. Not every study is designed the same way, and the design we choose changes what kind of conclusions we're allowed to draw from the results.
 
In this lesson, we'll look at the two broad categories of statistical studies: **observational studies**, where we simply watch and record what's already happening, and **experimental studies**, where we actively impose a treatment on subjects to see how they respond. Only a well-designed experiment can establish cause and effect — an observational study can only point to an association.
 
We'll also look at why the *design* of an experiment matters just as much as running it in the first place. If subjects or researchers know who is receiving a treatment, that knowledge can unintentionally influence the results. To guard against this, researchers use **blind** and **double-blind** studies, along with a **placebo** — a fake treatment that lets researchers separate the actual effect of a treatment from the effect of simply believing you're being treated.

## Lesson Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to accomplish the following:
- Distinguish between Observational and Experimental Studies and know when to use each type of study
- Determine when and how to implement blind studies
- Explain why placebos are used in experimental studies and how they help control for the placebo effect
