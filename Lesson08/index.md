## Lesson 8
Back in Lesson 4, we learned how to build a scatterplot to visualize the relationship between two quantitative variables. Some of those scatterplots showed a clear linear pattern — as one variable increased, the other tended to increase or decrease in a fairly straight line. In this lesson, we're going to turn that visual pattern into something we can calculate.
 
We'll start by fitting a **line of best fit** directly through a scatterplot, then use that line to make **predictions** for values we haven't observed — being careful about the difference between predicting inside our data (interpolation) and predicting outside it (extrapolation). From there, we'll calculate the **correlation coefficient**, a single number that tells us how strong and in what direction a linear relationship is. Finally, we'll look at the **coefficient of determination**, which tells us how much of the variation in one variable is actually explained by the other.
 
## Lesson Objectives
 
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to accomplish the following:
 
- Find the equation of the line of best fit for a set of paired data, using technology
- Use a line of best fit to make predictions, and explain the difference between interpolation and extrapolation
- Calculate and interpret the correlation coefficient $r$, including its sign (direction) and magnitude (strength)
- Calculate and interpret the coefficient of determination $r^2$ as the proportion of variation explained by the linear model
