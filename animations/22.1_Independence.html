Paired vs. Independent Samples

MATH 1040, Lesson 22.1

The big question

Paired or independent?

Before we can compare two samples, we need to know how they are related to each other.

That relationship decides which formulas we use, so it’s worth pausing to review the difference.

Move through the lesson with Next and Back, or the ← → keys. Take as long as you need on each slide.

Definition

Independent samples

Two samples are independent when the individuals in one sample have no natural relationship to the individuals in the other.

The two groups are made up of different individuals, selected separately. The groups don’t even need to be the same size.

    Definition

    Dependent (paired) samples

    Two samples are dependent, or paired, when each individual in one sample is naturally matched with exactly one individual in the other.

    Because every value has a partner, we can subtract each pair. The orange numbers show each difference, d = first − second.

    Pick an example

      How pairing happens

      Where pairs come from

      Pairing usually happens in one of two ways.

      • The same individuals are measured twiceFor example, before and after a treatment.
      • Individuals are matched by shared characteristicsFor example, twins, or the left hand and right hand of the same person.

      Why it matters

      Different data, different formulas

      The formulas for confidence intervals and hypothesis tests are different for dependent and independent samples.

      Independent

      No pairs to subtract, so each group keeps its own size and its own sample mean.

      Two separate means from groups of different sizes, so we need two-sample formulas.
